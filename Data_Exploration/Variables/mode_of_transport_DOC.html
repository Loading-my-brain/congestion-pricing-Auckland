Exploratory Spatial Data Analysis: Commuting Modes in Auckland (2023)

1. Data Documentation & Validation

From the previous step (SA2-part2.R), I saved the validated and processed data to “travel-to-work” from the 2023 Census at the Statistical Area 2 (SA2) level for Auckland (\(N = 504\) spatial units). The variables represent the proportions of commuters utilizing specific transit modes, calculated against the total stated population per neighborhood in Auckland High Urban Area.

Data Cleaning & Validation Anomalies

During the initial data validation phase, I identified two critical anomalies: * Identity Match: The total commuting population matches the total stated population (transportMode_total == transportMode_total_stated) across all neighborhoods. The proportion of commuters categorized under not_elsewhere_included was exactly zero across the entire study area. * The Missing Cohort (did_not_go_to_work_today): The variable prop_transport_did_not_go_to_work_today yielded \(504\) missing values (NA). This suggests that either the raw census category was unpopulated in this shapefile split or the values did not migrate successfully during the initial proportion calculation. I will flag this variable as unusable for subsequent modeling.

Definition of Aggregated Mode Share Variables

To make the analysis statistically manageable, I aggregated the raw transit categories into four key travel mode proportions (I’m hoping to use PT mode share as my response variable for the GWR model):

  1. Private Car Proportions (prop_aggregate_private_car): Combines commuters driving a personal vehicle (drive_a_private_car_truck_or_van) and those driving a company vehicle (drive_a_company_car_truck_or_van). Note, passngers aren’t included
  2. Public Transport Proportions (prop_aggregate_public_transport): Aggregates mass-transit modes, specifically public_bus, train, and ferry.
  3. Active Mode Proportions (prop_aggregate_active_modes): Aggregates non-motorized, active transit types: walking/jogging (walk_or_jog) and bicycling (bicycle).
  4. Work at Home Proportions (prop_aggregate_work_at_home): Represents the share of the working population avoiding a physical commute (work_at_home).

2. Statistical Findings & Academic Analysis

Descriptive Statistics of Mode Shares

The regional transit baseline in Auckland is heavily dominated by private vehicle dependency:

  • Private Vehicles (Drive): Private vehicle usage is the overwhelming primary mode share. On average, the baseline private car share is \(\mu = 55.5\%\) (\(\text{Median} = 57.4\%\)). However, when accounting for company-provided vehicles, the combined private car share reaches approximately \(64.4\%\) across the region.
  • Working from Home (WFH): WFH represents a major structural shift in the labor force, with a regional mean of \(\mu = 17.0\%\) (\(\text{Median} = 16.9\%\), \(\text{Max} = 31.3\%\)).
  • Public Transport (PT) & Active Modes: Public transit uptake remains consistently low. Public bus usage averages only \(\mu = 6.2\%\) (\(\text{Median} = 5.2\%\)), while rail infrastructure serves a minor fraction of commuters (\(\mu = 1.9\%\), \(\text{Median} = 1.3\%\)). Active transit is highly localized; pedestrian commuting (walk/jog) averages \(\mu = 3.9\%\) but exhibits a stark maximum of \(46.9\%\) in dense urban pockets.

Statistical Modeling: Repeated Measures ANOVA

To evaluate whether the differences in regional shares across the five primary travel modes (Private Car, Public Transport, Active Modes, Work at Home, and Other) are statistically meaningful, I executed a one-way Repeated Measures ANOVA, treating the SA2 neighborhoods as the subjects.

The test results indicate highly statistically significant differences between the transit mode shares across Auckland:

\[\text{F}(4, 1984) = 5235, \quad p < 2.2 \times 10^{-16}\]

The exceptionally high \(F\)-statistic confirms that the variation in mode choice across neighborhoods is not random. The regional shares are structurally distinct, heavily weighted toward private vehicular transport.

4. Methodological Pivot: Proportion vs. Density

A major component of my spatial analysis is evaluating the difference between transit proportions (relative shares) and transit densities (absolute commuters per square kilometer). This distinction is critical for network-level planning.

\[\begin{aligned} \text{Private Car:} && \text{Mean Share} = 55.5\% && \text{Mean Density} = 1,102 \text{ vehicles/km}^2 \ (\text{Max} = 5,164) \\ \text{Public Transport:} && \text{Mean Share} = 8.4\% && \text{Mean Density} = 193 \text{ people/km}^2 \ (\text{Max} = 5,117) \\ \text{Active Modes:} && \text{Mean Share} = 3.9\% && \text{Mean Density} = 184 \text{ people/km}^2 \ (\text{Max} = 10,755) \end{aligned}\]

To visualize this relationship, I mapped the combined commuter densities across Auckland:

The Spatial Paradox

  • Active Modes: While active transit has a tiny regional mean proportion (\(\mu = 3.9\%\)), its density peaks at an extraordinary \(10,755\) commuters/km² in dense inner-city pockets. This demonstrates that walking and cycling are highly geographically concentrated.
  • Private Cars: Some low-density fringe suburbs exhibit a car proportion of over \(70\%\), yet their car density is incredibly low (e.g., \(<100\) cars/km²). Conversely, high-density areas near the CBD might have a low car proportion (\(\sim15\%\)), but because of sheer density, they still generate over \(5,000\) cars/km².

Conclusion: Modeling only the percentage share of transit modes hides the actual pressure points on Auckland’s road networks. My research must evaluate both spatial metrics to avoid misrepresenting transit demand and congestion pressure.

5. Other observations

Analyzing the summaries of public transit shares prompted me to investigate exactly where and why we observe \(0\%\) participation. I generated an interactive map isolating neighborhoods with zero transit usage:

This map reveals that the zero-transit areas are largely clustered in peripheral, semi-rural SA2s. This spatial isolation is a direct manifestation of car dependency. The complete absence of alternative travel modes leaves these populations highly vulnerable to fuel price shocks and isolates households without access to private vehicles.

The WFH Shift

At \(17.0\%\) of the stated workforce, WFH has surpassed the combined total of public transport and active commuting (\(\sim12.3\%\)). This represents a fundamental post-pandemic shift. The “commuter” is no longer just moving between physical locations, which changes how we model peak-hour congestion and the spatial economy of Auckland’s outer suburbs.

Some reflections

  • Transit share vs Transit densities; num of obs/ total stated vs num of obs/ Land area.

    • Transit densities (commuters per square kilometer) are highly effective for visualizing the physical pressure on Auckland’s infrastructure, I am choosing to use mode share as the dependent variables in my GWR model. WHy? Using absolute densities in a spatial regression framework introduces a confounding factor: because dense urban areas naturally feature high volumes of all commuters, a density-based model would primarily predict general population density and urban form rather than actual transit preferences. By utilizing standardized proportions, I normalize for the varying population sizes of Auckland’s SA2s, isolating the actual behavioral choices of residents. This allows my GWR models to evaluate how localized socioeconomic drivers(such as income, home ownership, and education) determine the probability of a commuter choosing one mode over another, regardless of whether they reside in a low-density rural fringe or a highly populated inner-city apartment corridor.
