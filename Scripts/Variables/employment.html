Labor Force Dynamics & Travel Behavior

1. Data Documentation & Methodological Choices

I processed and validated the labor force variables from the 2023 Census at the SA2 level across Auckland (\(N = 504\)). These variables represent the baseline economic activity of the working-age population (aged 15 and over) and play a role in dictating the daily spatiotemporal constraints of commuters.

Constructing Standardized Labor Metrics

To make these variables analytically useful, I calculated four distinct metrics:

  1. Full-Time Employment Share (prop_emp_full_time): The proportion of the total working-age population employed full-time (\(\ge 30\) hours per week)
  2. Part-Time Employment Share (prop_emp_part_time): The proportion of the total working-age population employed part-time (\(< 30\) hours per week)
  3. Stated Unemployment Proportion (prop_unemployed_stated): The proportion of the total working-age population that is unemployed.
  4. Standardized Unemployment Rate (prop_unemployment_rate):

\[\text{Unemployment Rate} = \frac{\text{Unemployed}}{\text{Employed (FT + PT)} + \text{Unemployed}}\]

Methodological Justification: Rather than using the total working-age population as a blanket denominator, I calculated the standardized unemployment rate using the active labor force (employed + unemployed). This directly aligns with International Labour Organization (ILO) standards and standard policy frameworks used by the Ministry of Social Development (MSD). It filters out those who are “Not in the Labour Force” (e.g., retirees, full-time students, or homemakers), giving a much truer picture of localized economic distress.

2. Statistical Findings & Academic Analysis

Statistical Interpretation: Labor Force Variance & Post-Hoc Analysis

Interpreting the ANOVA Output (The Variance Breakdown)

The ANOVA splits the variance into two distinct levels (strata):

Stratum 1: Between-Neighborhoods (usual_residence_statistical_area_2_name_na)

This stratum looks at the overall baseline variation in labor shares across Auckland’s neighborhoods. * \[\text{F}(1, 500) = 417.2, \quad p < 2 \times 10^{-16}\] * Interpretation: This confirms that the overall level of economic activity is not uniform across Auckland. Some neighborhoods have structurally larger active labor forces (more workers overall), while others have larger retired or non-working populations. The difference between neighborhoods is highly statistically significant.

Stratum 2: Within-Neighborhoods (usual_residence_statistical_area_2_name_na:Labor_Status)

This stratum is where the actual repeated measures magic happens. It tests whether the proportions of the three labor states differ within the same neighborhoods. * \[\text{F}(2, 998) = 17,955, \quad p < 2 \times 10^{-16}\] * Interpretation: The astronomical \(F\)-statistic of \(17,955\) indicates a massive, highly significant difference. This tells me that within any given Auckland neighborhood, the shares of full-time, part-time, and unemployed individuals are not even close to being equal. Full-time employment systematically dwarfs part-time employment and unemployment across the region.

Interpreting the Post-Hoc Paired \(t\)-tests (The Pairwise Differences)

Because the ANOVA only tells us that “at least one group is different,” I ran pairwise paired \(t\)-tests to compare the three groups directly.

To prevent Type I error inflation (false positives) from running multiple tests on the same data, I used the Bonferroni correction. This is the most conservative adjustment method, multiplying the raw \(p\)-values by the number of comparisons (three) [1].

The output matrix is incredibly stark:

Comparison Adjusted \(p\)-value Significance
Part-Time vs. Full-Time \(< 2 \times 10^{-16}\) Extremely Significant
Unemployed vs. Full-Time \(< 2 \times 10^{-16}\) Extremely Significant
Unemployed vs. Part-Time \(< 2 \times 10^{-16}\) Extremely Significant

Every single pairwise comparison is highly statistically significant. Across Auckland:

  1. Full-time employment is systematically and significantly larger than both part-time employment and unemployment.
  2. Part-time employment is systematically and significantly larger than unemployment.

What This Actually Means for Transport Behavior

These results are crucial for justifying the next steps in my research. Because these three labor cohorts are statistically distinct across Auckland, we cannot treat them as a monolith in transport modeling.

If these groups had similar distributions, we could aggregate them. But because they are so vastly different, they represent three completely different sets of spatial and temporal constraints: * The Full-Time Cohort: Represents the dominant, rigid, peak-hour commute. Their massive statistical presence explains why Auckland’s transport network experiences severe congestion during traditional morning and evening peaks.

  • The Part-Time Cohort: Represents a smaller but highly distinct group that does not fit standard peak-hour transit models. They require off-peak service frequency and suburban-to-suburban connections due to their household duties and varied hours.

  • The Unemployed Cohort: Represents a marginalized spatial group. Because their proportions are so small compared to the working population, their transit needs are often ignored by planners who focus primarily on heavy commute corridors. This reinforces their spatial isolation and lock-out from the job market

3. Reflections: Time Poverty and Transport Behavior

Honestly, looking at these labor variables alongside our transport modes brings up some of the most critical structural insights of this project.

Theory #1: The Time-Poverty Trap and the 40+ Hour Commuter

  • We need to talk about “time poverty.” It is not just an academic buzzword; it is a direct driver of car dependency.

  • Commuters working long hours, specifically those in the \(40\text{--}49\), \(50\text{--}59\), and \(60+\) hours per week brackets, face a massive deficit of free time.

    • I was thinking of using these 3 groups (hours of working) instead of part time vs full-time. But to make my life easier, I stuck to using employement status instead
  • When your workweek is that demanding, you literally do not have the luxury to spend 45 minutes walking to a train station, waiting on a platform, and transferring to a bus. In Auckland, where peripheral-to-hub public transit is notoriously slow, driving is often the only viable way to squeeze any personal time out of the day. For these time-poor workers, the high marginal cost of driving (parking, petrol, tolls) is a price they are forced to pay to buy back their own time.

Part-Time Workers and “Trip-Chaining”

“Trip chaining is the practice of combining multiple out-of-home destinations and errands into a single continuous journey instead of making separate round trips. A common example is leaving home, stopping at a grocery store and a daycare on the way to work, and then picking up mail on the way back home.”

On the flip side, we have part-time workers (prop_emp_part_time). While they might have more flexible hours, they face a different kind of transport trap: trip-chaining. * Part-time workers are frequently balancing employment with unpaid activities, such as looking after children or performing household work (cooking, repairs, gardening). * Trying to combine a work trip with a school run and a grocery stop using Auckland’s rigid, radial public transport network is virtually impossible. This cohort is heavily pushed into car use because our transit network is built for simple “home-to-work-and-back” commuting, completely failing those with complex, multi-stop daily schedules.

The Spatial Exclusion of the Unemployed

  • The standard unemployment rate (prop_unemployment_rate) is a powerful indicator of spatial exclusion. High unemployment rates are heavily clustered in the low-wealth spatial cold spots we identified in South and West Auckland.

  • If you do not own a reliable car and live in a transit desert, your job search is physically limited to a very small geographic radius.

  • Most of Auckland’s employment growth is concentrated in specific industrial/commercial hubs (Workplace Address clusters) that are incredibly difficult to reach from residential fringes without a car. This creates a brutal feedback loop: spatial isolation breeds unemployment, and unemployment prevents the accumulation of capital needed to escape spatial isolation.
