Individual Home Ownership Analysis Documentation

1. Data Documentation & Validation

I processed and validated the individual home ownership variables from the 2023 Census at the SA2 level for Auckland (\(N = 504\) spatial units). In the NZ Census, individual home ownership measures whether an individual person owns their usual residence (either outright, with a mortgage, or through a family trust) or does not own it.

Home ownership could be useful for the GWR model (E[PT mode share]) as this individual-level metric could be useful for modeling personal travel choices, as property ownership status is a proxy for individual wealth, age, and long-term residential stability. BUT! collinearity should be checked

Variable Definitions and Transformations

I cleaned and renamed the raw census columns into a standardized format: * prop_homeownership_owners: The proportion of individuals who own, partly own, or hold their home in a family trust. * prop_homeownership_renters: The proportion of individuals who do not own their home and do not hold it in a family trust (the standard census proxy for renters). * density_homeownership_owners: The spatial density of homeowners (calculated as owners per unit area). * density_homeownership_renters: The spatial density of renters (calculated as renters per unit area).

2. Statistical Findings & Academic Analysis

Descriptive Statistics

The descriptive summary reveals that at an individual level, the majority of Auckland’s neighborhood populations are non-homeowners (renters):

  • Renter Proportions: The regional mean for individual renters is \(\mu = 59.9\%\) (\(\text{Median} = 59.2\%\)). In highly transient or industrial neighborhoods, the renter share reaches \(100\%\).
  • Owner Proportions: Individual homeowners account for a regional mean of \(\mu = 40.2\%\) (\(\text{Median} = 40.8\%\)), peaking at \(70.2\%\) in high-wealth, low-density suburbs.

Statistical Modeling: Paired \(t\)-test

To mathematically confirm the difference between individual-level ownership and rental profiles across Auckland, I executed a standard paired \(t\)-test comparing prop_homeownership_owners and prop_homeownership_renters directly across the \(504\) neighborhoods:

\[\text{t}(502) = -16.971, \quad p < 2.2 \times 10^{-16}\]

The mean difference is \(-19.7\%\) (95% CI: \([-22.0\%, -17.4\%]\)).

Academic Interpretation: There is an exceptionally strong, statistically significant difference between individual homeowner and renter shares across Auckland. Across the region, the individual renter share is approximately \(20\) percentage points higher than the homeowner share.

(Note: Because this is individual-level data rather than household-level, this reflects the reality of Auckland’s housing market where large numbers of working-age adults, flatmates, and multi-generational family members do not personally own the property they live in, even if the household is owner-occupied).

4. Honours Research Reflections on Transport Behavior

Honestly, mapping individual homeownership is where the structural transport story starts to get real. Linking tenure status to travel choices exposes the deep inequalities built into Auckland’s geography:

  • The Waitākere Car Dependency Trap: Suburbs like Titirangi, Laingholm, and Waima have some of the highest homeowner shares in the region. But they are physically isolated, hilly, and low-density. Homeowners here are almost entirely locked into car use because traditional public transit or cycling infrastructure is physically difficult to implement in the bush-clad Waitākere Ranges. Here, homeownership directly correlates with high car dependency.
  • The High-Deprivation Renter Precarity: In industrial fringes like Ōtāhuhu Industrial or Sylvia Park, we see renter shares hitting up to \(100\%\). These residents are often lower-income shift workers living in car-centric, industrial environments. They face severe transport precarity: they have the lowest financial access to private cars, yet they live in areas with virtually no safe walking, cycling, or public transport options outside of peak hours.
  • The Transit-Amenity Privilege: The high renter densities in the CBD (Parnell, Symonds Street, Anzac Avenue) align perfectly with our public transport and active mode outliers. These renters are geographically privileged—they can afford to live in transit-rich areas where they can easily bypass car ownership.
  • The Suburban Tenant Gap: In contrast, renters in Avondale and Glen Innes live in high densities but must navigate suburban transit deficits. They have high rental costs but limited transport choices, making them highly vulnerable to transport poverty.
